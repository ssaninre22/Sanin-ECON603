\section{ Very Preliminary Analysis}

\noindent\textbf{Definitions and current limitations.} The preliminary analysis relies on a provisional definition of college towns, reflecting the ongoing data construction described in the introduction. Ideally, I would classify local areas using an endogenous measure of university exposure based on the share of employment or payroll in higher education, constructed from detailed QCEW industry data and merged with IPEDS student and staffing information. Because the public QCEW all-in-one files suppress industry-level employment for public institutions for several years, assembling these data requires manual extraction from individual county and MSA files. This process is underway but not yet complete.

For the moment, I adopt an external classification from \citet{GumprechtBook}, who identifies 305 U.S. cities where four-year college enrollment exceeds 20 percent of the local population and that satisfy additional criteria related to size and geographic separation. I map these cities to their counties and metropolitan areas and classify an area as a college town if it contains at least one such city. This definition is coarse: counties often include multiple cities with heterogeneous structures, and MSAs may expand well beyond the immediate university footprint. Nonetheless, the two geographic levels offer different tradeoffs. Counties provide a large sample size and finer local variation, while MSAs better approximate functional labor markets and align more closely with available QCEW employment and payroll statistics. As a result, I report results for both units, with a preference for MSAs in the full analysis once the endogenous exposure measure is available.

\noindent\textbf{Descriptive patterns.} Figure~\ref{fig:unemprates} presents seasonally adjusted unemployment rates for college-town and non-college areas from 1990 to 2024 at the county and MSA levels. Across the entire period, college-town areas exhibit lower median unemployment rates than non-college areas, although non-college areas display greater dispersion. During the 2001 and 2008 recessions, the gap narrows but does not reverse. In the COVID recession, unemployment spikes sharply everywhere, yet college-town MSAs continue to show slightly lower medians than their non-college counterparts. These patterns suggest that, under this preliminary classification, college towns do not exhibit heightened cyclical fragility and may display modestly lower unemployment levels in normal times.

\noindent\textbf{Event-study patterns around recessions.} Figure~\ref{fig:event_study_college_towns} shows preliminary event-study estimates of the differential response of unemployment in college-town and non-college areas around NBER recessions. All specifications include area and time fixed effects and cluster standard errors at the corresponding geographic level. For counties, college-town areas experience somewhat larger unemployment increases in the full sample, but this pattern is driven almost entirely by the COVID recession. When the COVID period is excluded, differential effects shrink and become statistically indistinguishable from zero. At the MSA level, which better aligns with commuting zones and sectoral integration, the full-sample estimates show little difference, while the pre-COVID estimates indicate that college-town MSAs experience slightly smaller unemployment increases during recessions. This is consistent with a modest stabilizing effect of universities during downturns in which campus activity remains largely in person.

These patterns are preliminary and reflect the limitations of the external classification of college towns. Once the endogenous exposure measure is constructed using QCEW and IPEDS, the next stage of the analysis will re-estimate these dynamics and extend them to sectoral employment, payroll, and housing outcomes using the synthetic difference in differences, interaction difference in differences, and local projection methods described in the empirical design.
